Section 1 of 7

Building your module without making a mess of the LMS

~1.5 min read

For most of your students, the LMS is the module. It's where they find out what's expected, what's due, and whether anyone is there. A well-built module page quietly supports learning. A messy one adds confusion, anxiety and a flood of emails to you.

In the next thirty minutes, we'll build a module space that's clear, accessible and engaging. We'll look at the module page, announcements and content organisation, then forums, live links and communication, then the tools that support peer and lecturer–student interaction. We'll finish with using Turnitin for assessment.

By the end of this unit, you'll be able to:

  • Plan a clear, accessible and engaging module space
  • Organise a module page, content and announcements so students can navigate with confidence
  • Design forums, live sessions and communication routines that support learning
  • Select LMS tools for peer and lecturer–student interaction that match your outcomes
  • Set up Turnitin links for formative and summative assessment at Levels 7, 8 and 9

Moodle and Blackboard are examples, not requirements. I'll use both throughout, but the principles apply to any LMS, although the menu names will likely differ.

Pitched at NFQ Level 8. This unit is written for academic staff new to curriculum design, with Level 7 and Level 9 contrasts where the advice genuinely differs.

You're the expert on your discipline, so throughout I'll ask you to draw on your industry experience: how professionals communicate, collaborate and judge quality online. Have a notepad ready, or use the reflection boxes.

Section 2 of 7

A clear, accessible and engaging online learning space

~5.5 min read

Start with the student's view.

Imagine opening your module for the first time on a phone, on a bus, after a long day at work. At Level 8 you're likely teaching many adult learners with jobs and families, so that's a realistic picture.

  1. 1ClearLess to read, click and decode
  2. 2AccessibleWorks for every student, by design
  3. 3EngagingA human voice, and something to think about

Clear first

Sweller's cognitive load theory (1988) reminds us that working memory is limited. Every unnecessary click, duplicate file or ambiguous label uses up attention that should go to learning. Mayer's multimedia principles (2009) say the same about visuals: remove decoration that doesn't help. So, aim for a consistent layout, predictable names, and one obvious place to start.

The design principle is alignment. It will be familiar from Biggs and Tang (2011). Your module space should make the learning outcomes, the learning activities and the assessments visible and connected. If students can't see how this week's activity relates to the assessment, the structure is hiding the point.

    Now accessible

    This isn't an optional extra. Public bodies in Ireland, including universities, are bound by the EU Web Accessibility Directive, which points to the Web Content Accessibility Guidelines, WCAG, as the standard. More importantly, it's good design for everyone. CAST's Universal Design for Learning guidelines (2018) encourage multiple ways of engaging, representing content, and expressing learning.

    In practice, that's a short list:

    • Use real headings, not bold text, so screen readers can navigate.
    • Write meaningful link text, such as “Assessment brief for Report 1”, rather than “click here”.
    • Add alt text to images that carry meaning.
    • Caption and transcribe video and audio.
    • Check colour contrast.
    • Prefer web pages or accessible documents to scanned PDFs.

    Let a tool do the first pass. Your editor may have a built-in accessibility checker, and many institutions provide a tool such as Ally. Use it.

    Finally, engaging

    Garrison, Anderson and Archer's Community of Inquiry framework (2000) describes three kinds of presence:

    Teaching presence
    Your design, facilitation and direction.
    Social presence
    Students feeling they're among people.
    Cognitive presence
    Real thinking and enquiry.

    A module page that is only a document repository delivers none of these. Engagement comes from a visible human voice, such as a short welcome video, from spaces to interact, and from tasks that need students to think and apply.

    The National Forum's digital teaching and learning work, including its roadmap for enhancement in a digital world (2015), encourages us to use technology purposefully to support learning rather than for its own sake.

    The question is never “which tools can I add?” It's “what should students do, and what's the simplest way to support it?”

    Section 3 of 7

    The module page, announcements and content organisation

    ~6 min read

    Let's build the page. Think of it in three layers: a Start Here area, a structure for the learning content, and announcements to keep things moving.

    Put this at the top of the page, before anything else:

    • a short welcome, ideally with a brief video or audio message;
    • the learning outcomes;
    • an at-a-glance assessment schedule with dates and weightings;
    • how and when to contact you;
    • where to ask questions; and
    • links to key supports such as the library, academic writing and disability services.

    If students have to hunt for these, they email you instead.

    Choose one logical pattern and use it consistently. Common options are a weekly structure, a topic structure, or an assessment-led structure. Weekly works well for fully online modules. Topic structure fits when content doesn't map neatly to weeks.

    Whatever you choose, repeat the same internal pattern in every section. For example: a short overview, what to do, the resources, an activity, and what's next.

    Predictability reduces effort.

    Announcements are the third layer — how you keep things moving once the semester is under way. We'll look at them properly in a moment, after a quick sort.

    Practical rules of thumb for structure

    • Chunk content into manageable pieces rather than one long scroll. In Moodle, use topic sections and the course index; in Blackboard, use learning modules or folders.
    • Prefer a web page to a stack of attachments, and link to a single file rather than uploading copies.
    • Use clear naming conventions, such as “Week 3: Activity: Case analysis” rather than “Notes final2”.
    • Use access restrictions and completion tracking to guide students through a sequence — called restrict access and activity completion in Moodle, and release conditions in Blackboard — but use them sparingly, because over-restriction frustrates adult learners who dip in and out.
    • Hide or remove anything unused. If you roll a module over from last year, check dates, links and references to old assessments.

    Decide where each item belongs — drag it, or select it and then choose a box. Some could reasonably go in two places, so think about which is its primary home.

    Announcements

    They're your most powerful, most underused tool.

    In Moodle, the Announcements forum tends to be one-way and typically sends an email to students. In Blackboard, announcements can also be emailed. Because they're pushed to students, use them well.

    • Keep announcements short, specific and purposeful.
    • A weekly “what's happening” post builds rhythm and presence: what we covered, what's coming, and what to do.
    • Use announcements for time-sensitive information, not as a place for permanent content.
    • Post at a predictable time, so students know when to look.

    Quick tip: a sample weekly announcement.

    Week 4: This week we move from describing to evaluating. Read the two short articles and post your first response in the Week 4 forum by Thursday. Live session Friday at 10:00; the link is in Start Here. Reminder: Report 1 draft is due in two weeks. Aim to post questions in the Q&A forum so everyone benefits.

    Section 4 of 7

    Discussion forums, live links and communication

    ~5.5 min read

    The aim is to cut down on scattered emails and build a predictable pattern students can rely on.

    Start with forums

    Forums are the backbone of asynchronous interaction. Two or three well-designed forums beat ten unused ones. A typical set is a Q&A forum for module questions, a discussion forum tied to learning tasks, and perhaps a social space.

    In Moodle, there are several forum types: a standard forum for open discussion; a Q&A forum, where students post their own response before seeing others', which prevents copying and encourages individual thinking; and others for single topics. Blackboard offers discussion boards, with options to organise by topic or group.

    For module questions

    Answer common questions once, publicly

    A Q&A forum answers common questions once, publicly. Encourage students to answer each other, and step in when needed. This reduces the email load and builds community.

    Tied to a learning task

    It needs a good prompt

    A prompt that asks “What did you think of the reading?” produces a handful of one-line replies. A prompt that asks students to apply, compare or evaluate produces real thinking. Pitch it to the level:

    Level 7
    Ask students to apply a concept to a defined scenario.
    Level 8
    Ask them to evaluate competing approaches and justify a position using evidence, and to respond critically to a peer.
    Level 9
    Invite students to lead: to critique a research paper, pose a question for the group, or synthesise the discussion for others.
    Optional

    Somewhere to get to know each other

    A social space gives students somewhere to chat that isn't the Q&A forum. It supports the social presence we met in Section 2, and keeps the Q&A forum for questions.

    Set expectations

    • Say how many posts, by when, and what a good post looks like.
    • Agree simple netiquette.
    • Decide how you'll participate.
    • If the discussion is assessed, use a rubric and share it in advance.

    Salmon's five-stage model of e-moderating (2011) is helpful for deciding how you take part. Early on, you support access and community. Later, you step back as students build knowledge together.

    Live links

    Whether you use Teams, Zoom, Blackboard Collaborate or another tool, the principles are the same.

    • Keep one persistent link in Start Here, rather than sending new links each week.
    • Publish a schedule and say clearly whether attendance is required.
    • Record sessions if policy allows, and caption them, so students who can't attend aren't left behind.
    • Share a short agenda.
    • Plan interaction: polls, breakout rooms and short tasks, not a long monologue.

    Time zones and work patterns matter. Offering an alternative to the live session, such as a recorded version plus a forum task, supports adult learners.

    Set your communication policy

    Put it in Start Here. Say where students should ask questions, how quickly you'll reply — for example within two working days — and what you'll use email for. Clarity here protects your time as much as it helps students.

    LMS tools for peer and lecturer–student interaction

    ~5 min read

    Moore (1989) described three types of interaction in distance education: learner–content, learner–learner, and learner–instructor. A strong module includes all three.

    Laurillard's conversational framework (2012) goes further: learning happens through an ongoing dialogue, in which students act, receive feedback, and adjust. So, choose tools that create that loop.

    The tools for each type of interaction

    Learner–content

    Prompt students to engage with the material actively

    • Interactive content
    • Short quizzes with immediate feedback
    • Embedded video with questions
    MoodleBlackboard
    Quizzes, lessons and H5P interactive content.
    Tests and interactive options, depending on version.
    Keep theseLow-stakes, and linked to outcomes.
    Learner–learner

    Several options

    • Forums for discussion.
    • Groups, so students can work in smaller teams, with private group forums or spaces.
    • Collaborative documents or wikis for co-creating content.
    • Peer review. Students submit work, review each other's using a rubric, and receive feedback.
    MoodleBlackboard
    The Workshop activity.
    Peer review options too, depending on your version.

    Peer review builds evaluative judgement, and it works especially well when you give students the criteria and a model example. At Level 8, this might be peer review of a draft report; at Level 9, a research proposal.

    Learner–instructor

    Beyond announcements and forums

    • A short mid-module feedback survey to find out what's working.
    • The assignment tool for timely written, audio or video feedback tied to the rubric.
    • Bookable office hours or a regular drop-in link.
    • The activity data your LMS provides, which can help you spot students who haven't logged in. That allows early, supportive outreach.
    MoodleBlackboard
    Completion and activity reports.
    Course activity tools.
    Be transparentTell students what data you view and why, and follow your institution's data protection policy.

    Feedback is a conversation. The National Forum's work on feedback and assessment (2017) stresses dialogue: feedback as a conversation rather than a one-way report. LMS tools make that conversation easier, but only if you plan for it.

    Build your interaction map

    Try it for a sample module. Pick one tool for each type of interaction, then compare with the suggested answer. There's no single right answer. The test is whether each choice supports a stated outcome.

    Interaction map · Level 8 Project Management
    The module · sample outcomes
    • Evaluate competing project management methodologies for a given organisational context.
    • Produce, and justify, a risk register for a real project.

    One defensible answer

    Student–contentAn embedded video of a worked case, with questions at key points. It makes students apply and judge as they watch, and ties straight to the first outcome.
    Student–studentPeer review of a draft risk register, using the rubric. It builds evaluative judgement and supports the second outcome. The forum task is just as defensible for the first.
    Student–lecturerRubric-based feedback through the assignment tool. It closes the loop: students act, receive feedback, and adjust.

    Your picks may differ and still be right. Ask of each one: what should students do, and does this tool support it?

    Using Turnitin links for assessments

    ~4.5 min read

    Finally, assessment submission. Turnitin is widely used for submitting written work, checking similarity to other sources, and marking and giving feedback.

    Depending on your institution, it appears in Moodle and Blackboard as an integrated assignment or a link. Check your local guidance, as set-ups vary.

    First, a mindset point: the similarity report is not a plagiarism verdict. It shows where text matches other sources. Matches in a reference list, quotations or common phrases are normal. A human has to interpret the report. Used well, it's a learning tool: it helps students understand citation and paraphrasing, and gives you a starting point for conversation.

    Set up Turnitin well

    Label it “Report 1: Draft (formative)” or “Report 1: Final (summative)”, not just “Assignment 1”. Students shouldn't have to guess.

    Put a short summary of the brief, word count, file types, referencing style and the deadline on the submission page. Link to the full brief and the rubric.

    Decide on the due date, whether late submission is allowed, and how many submissions are permitted.

    • For drafts, allow resubmission before the deadline so students can see their similarity report and improve their citation. It's a powerful formative opportunity.
    • For final submission, you'd normally restrict resubmission.

    Decide whether to exclude the bibliography or quoted material from the report, and be consistent.

    Use a rubric attached to the assignment, reusable comments, and where available audio comments. Return grades and feedback on the date you promised.

    Before the deadline, check the link works, the dates display properly and your instructions make sense. And have a plan for students with difficulty submitting, such as an alternative route to a lecturer or administrator.

    Here's how it might look across levels

    Ordinary Bachelor Degree

    Teach referencing and paraphrasing

    A draft submission of a short applied report, with the similarity report used to teach referencing and paraphrasing, then a final submission graded against a structured rubric.

    Honours Bachelor Degree

    Peer feedback, then a weighted final

    A draft literature review or proposal submitted with peer feedback, followed by a weighted final report. Students interpret their own similarity report in a short reflection, which develops academic integrity as a professional skill.

    Master's Degree

    A dialogue about source use

    Larger documents such as thesis chapters or research proposals, submitted for supervisor feedback. The report supports a dialogue about source use and synthesis, rather than acting as a checkpoint.

    Match the tool to the task

    Turnitin suits written submissions. If you've designed an authentic assessment, such as a portfolio, a presentation or a practical, use the assignment tool or another submission format that fits the work, rather than forcing it into a text-matching tool.

    Generative AI is a related concern. Turnitin and some other tools offer AI-writing indicators, but these are unreliable and can produce false positives. SETU has turned off the AI detection tool in Turnitin because it was deemed to be so unreliable. Ireland's National Academic Integrity Network and QQI guidelines (2023) emphasise transparency and assessment design over detection alone.

    Section 7 of 7

    Summary and final reflection

    ~2 min read

    Let's pull it together.
    A clear, accessible, engaging space
    Design from the student's view. Reduce cognitive load, follow accessibility basics, and build teaching, social and cognitive presence.
    Module page and announcements
    Start Here, a consistent structure, clear names, and short, purposeful announcements.
    Forums, live links and communication
    A few well-designed forums, prompts pitched to the level, one persistent live link, and a clear communication policy.
    Peer and lecturer–student interaction
    Plan for all three types of interaction and choose tools that serve your outcomes.
    Turnitin
    One clearly labelled link per assessment, deliberate settings, drafts for learning, and human judgement on the report.
